FUSION 2026 · Keynote 2 · the whole flow, end to end · merged 25 Aug

Map it. Build it. Run it. Improve it.

Somebody bought a very expensive machine and it broke. They want it fixed under warranty. Their production line is down while they wait, so every hour costs them money.

That is the whole scenario, and this is the whole flow: Max's Cartographer beats and the Maestro Case segment merged into one document, per the 25 Aug alignment call. It happens at Cobalt Ridge Automation, a fictional industrial-equipment manufacturer, and the machine is a conveyor system that sorts packages for shipping at a customer's distribution center. One warranty claim carries all four acts: nobody has written the process down yet, so it gets mapped; the map gets built into something that runs; it runs mostly on its own; and what people decided while it ran makes both the process and the map better.

The flow in briefplain language, no screens needed

Map the work

  1. The map starts genuinely empty — the domains exist because the org chart does, and there are four ways to begin.
  2. Day 0: Maestro hands over the three processes that already run, four candidates arrive as seeds, and warranty resolution is the empty slot. This is the picture the demo comes back to at the end.
  3. Rather than start from nothing, start from a process UiPath has already mapped and had reviewed.
  4. The project opens with everything attached: five sources the analysis will cite line by line (one in conflict, one that nothing cites), ten stakeholders, four declared deliverables.
  5. One prompt, and the analysis assembles itself. It asks the one thing no document could answer.
  6. The map is honest about what it does not know. One step decides something and states no rule. Another records nothing at all.
  7. The map draws itself — two department flows stitched into one diagram, with the undocumented step drawn as a gap rather than smoothed over.
  8. The gap the documents cannot close goes to the person who owns it — four questions, answered the same day. Her answer that nothing gets recorded is the finding.
  9. The people who own the work correct it. Coverage sits with warranty operations, not field service. Substitution needs a standard, not just an approver. Two failures on the same machine inside a year should stop a case closing.
  10. The corrected design publishes as one versioned, signed record with an owner, a risk tier, a compliance scope and three constraints no build may change, so the handoff can be audited.

Build it

  1. A coding agent reads it and builds a case, not a folder of activities, because the map showed this work waits on people and branches when things go wrong.
  2. The rules the reviewers wrote down are the rules the case agent now reads at run time. Then the business changes one, and the agent makes the change.

Run it

  1. 93 out of every 100 claims finish without anybody touching them.
  2. Sarah gets one of the rest. A part failed early, which is on us, and somebody also raised the torque limit past the approved envelope without sign-off, which is on them. Both are true, so neither full coverage nor a denial fits.
  3. She decides in a console with the evidence in front of her, and marks which of it actually helped. A few seconds, nothing held up.
  4. Later the customer sends photos, after coverage was already decided. Nobody routes them. The case agent reroutes to engineering on its own.
  5. The execution trail shows why it went there and who signed off. Engineering confirms, the machine is fixed, and the case closes.

Improve it

  1. Every decision is on the record, Sarah's included, and so is the same decision at other customers.
  2. Reading across them surfaces two patterns. A particular part under a small amount gets approved every time and the answer is never different. And repeat failures of that same part keep closing with nobody checking the part itself.
  3. Approve the first as a rule once and those claims stop waiting for a person. The second adds a check rather than removing one.
  4. That rule is now written down in the map. The map made the process, and the process improved the map.
  5. Ninety days later, the survey has run across the business. A hundred and thirty four processes, 47% of the work mapped, nine rules written down that had only ever lived in somebody's head — and warranty resolution, the empty dot we opened on, is the best mapped process here.
  6. Switch the lens and the map answers a different question: eleven processes have nobody accountable for them, which is why their exceptions leave the system instead of getting escalated.
  7. Twelve findings are open across the whole business, sorted by whether two procedures contradict each other, a system has drifted from the floor, or the telemetry knows before a person does.
  8. The VP of service opens it and gets a ranked list rather than a dashboard. The top item is not an automation at all: nobody owns the claim end to end.
  9. And it still says out loud what it has not got to. Fifty eight processes remain unmapped, listed as a queue with the ownerless ones first.

What's on screen, in order:

  1. Cartographer. The empty estate, the Use Case Explorer inside it, the analysis, and the map of work as it fills in and gets corrected.
  2. Cartographer, the published record. The design as a governed, signed, versioned entry — which surface hosts it (Cartographer or Automation Hub) is a Thursday question.
  3. Coding agent. Turning that record into a Maestro case.
  4. UiPath Studio. The case plan and the rules the case agent reads.
  5. Maestro Case App. The queue, Sarah's console, and the execution trail.
  6. Cartographer, Suggestions. The decision ledger, the suggestions it produces, and applying one as a rule.
  7. Cartographer, the estate at day 90. Six frames: the map of work by coverage and again by ownership, the same thing as a table, the twelve open findings, the ranked list the VP sees, and the backlog it has not got to.

All names, values and clocks are illustrative. Continuous improvement is shown as it will work, and is coming soon rather than shipping today. Cartographer and the verticalized processes are generally available. UiPath orchestrates the warranty response across teams, agents and systems; it does not control the physical equipment.

1. Executive Summary

Cobalt Ridge handles roughly 7,400 warranty claims a year. Global Warranty Operations owns the process end to end, with six other functions joining at named stages. The trigger is a customer-reported failure; the close-out is a restored line, a validated outcome, and a reconciled cost posting.

Time to resolution11.4 → 3.5 d Primary KPI, FY2027 target
First-time fix61 → 85%Secondary KPI
Annual claims~7,400Trailing 12 months

Six stages always run. Four more open only on an exception, which is what makes this a case rather than a straight line. Forty-four pieces of work sit across them: ten agent tasks, seven processes, seven API calls and twenty human decisions. Three constraints are marked as unable to change in any build: the coverage rationale is written, the recurrence gate blocks closure, and substitutions off the approved list go to engineering.
