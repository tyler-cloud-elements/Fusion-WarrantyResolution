Map it. Build it. Run it. Improve it.

Somebody bought a very expensive machine and it broke. They want it fixed under warranty. Their production line is down while they wait, so every hour costs them money.

That is the whole scenario, and this is the whole flow: Max's Cartographer beats and the Maestro Case segment merged into one document, per the 25 Aug alignment call. It happens at Cobalt Ridge Automation, a fictional industrial-equipment manufacturer, and the machine is a conveyor system that sorts packages for shipping at a customer's distribution center. One warranty claim carries all three acts: nobody has written the process down yet, it gets mapped and built, it runs mostly on its own, and then it gets better.

The flow in briefplain language, no screens needed

Build it

  1. Nothing has been surveyed. Maestro knows three processes because they run there, and warranty resolution is not one of them.
  2. Rather than start from nothing, start from a process UiPath has already mapped and had reviewed.
  3. One prompt plus the documents this company already had, and the analysis assembles itself. It asks the one thing no document could answer.
  4. The map is honest about what it does not know. One step decides something and states no rule. Another records nothing at all.
  5. The people who own the work correct it. Coverage sits with warranty operations, not field service. Substitution needs a standard, not just an approver. Two failures on the same machine inside a year should stop a case closing.
  6. The corrected design publishes as one versioned, signed record with an owner, a risk tier and a compliance scope, so the handoff can be audited.
  7. A coding agent reads it and builds a case, not a folder of activities, because the map showed this work waits on people and branches when things go wrong.
  8. The rules the reviewers wrote down are the rules the case agent now reads at run time. Then the business changes one, and the agent makes the change.

Run it

  1. 93 out of every 100 claims finish without anybody touching them.
  2. Sarah gets one of the rest. A part failed early, which is on us, and somebody also raised the torque limit past the approved envelope without sign-off, which is on them. Both are true, so neither full coverage nor a denial fits.
  3. She decides in a console with the evidence in front of her, and marks which of it actually helped. A few seconds, nothing held up.
  4. Later the customer sends photos, after coverage was already decided. Nobody routes them. The case agent reroutes to engineering on its own.
  5. The execution trail shows why it went there and who signed off. Engineering confirms, the machine is fixed, and the case closes.

Improve it

  1. Every decision is on the record, Sarah's included, and so is the same decision at other customers.
  2. Reading across them surfaces two patterns. A particular part under a small amount gets approved every time and the answer is never different. And repeat failures of that same part keep closing with nobody checking the part itself.
  3. Approve the first as a rule once and those claims stop waiting for a person. The second adds a check rather than removing one.
  4. That rule is now written down in the map. The map made the process, and the process improved the map.

What's on screen, in order:

  1. Cartographer. The empty estate, the Use Case Explorer inside it, the analysis, and the map of work as it fills in and gets corrected.
  2. Automation Hub. The design published as a governed, signed, versioned record.
  3. Coding agent. Turning that record into a Maestro case.
  4. UiPath Studio. The case plan and the rules the case agent reads.
  5. Maestro Case App. The queue, Sarah's console, and the execution trail.
  6. Cartographer, Suggestions. The decision ledger, the suggestions it produces, applying one, and the estate ninety days on.

All names, values and clocks are illustrative. Continuous improvement is shown as it will work, and is coming soon rather than shipping today. Cartographer and the verticalized processes are generally available. UiPath orchestrates the warranty response across teams, agents and systems; it does not control the physical equipment.

Open on Thursday: which opening we use (this one, the finished estate, or the Use Case Explorer), whether the Automation Hub governance screen stays, and the runtime, which nobody has budgeted yet. The act runtimes below are my estimate, not an agreed number.

1. Executive Summary

Cobalt Ridge handles roughly 7,400 warranty claims a year across industrial automation and material-handling equipment. Global Warranty Operations owns the process end to end, with Customer Support, Field Service, Parts and Logistics, Engineering, Quality and Finance joining at named stages. The trigger is a customer-reported failure through the hotline, email, portal or a technician; the close-out is a restored line, a validated customer outcome, and a reconciled cost posting.

Time to resolution11.4 → 3.5 d Primary KPI, FY2027 target
First-time fix61 → 85%Secondary KPI
Annual claims~7,400Trailing 12 months

Six stages always run. Four more open only on an exception, which is what makes this a case rather than a straight line. Forty-four pieces of work sit across them: ten agent tasks, seven processes, seven API calls and twenty human decisions.

Talk track

“The customer uploads photos of the failed drive through the portal, after coverage has already been decided. That upload becomes an event, and the event wakes the case agent, because new evidence can change a decision that has already been made. It checks the photos against the earlier finding and sends the case to engineering to confirm the cause. It does not decide the technical question itself, because confirming a defect from a photograph is a judgment call, not a rule. Nobody routed any of that.”
